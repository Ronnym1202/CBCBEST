// 01 · Definition

What is an Expected Learning Outcome (ELO)?

Official KICD Definition

An Expected Learning Outcome (ELO) is a clear, precise, and measurable statement that describes what a learner should be able to know, do, or value by the end of a lesson, unit, strand, or sub-strand — expressed in terms of observable learner behaviour, not teacher activity.

In Kenya's CBC framework, ELOs are written at the lesson level (Specific Learning Outcomes/SLOs) and at the curriculum design level (General Learning Outcomes/GLOs). KICD requires all ELOs to be learner-centred, observable, measurable, and achievable within the specified timeframe.

The Core Distinction: GLOs vs SLOs

GLO · General Learning Outcome

Broad statements written at strand or sub-strand level by KICD curriculum designers. They describe the overall competency targets for a subject across a grade band. Teachers do not write GLOs — they are provided in the curriculum design documents.

Example: "The learner should be able to apply algebraic reasoning to solve real-world problems."
SLO · Specific Learning Outcome

Precise, lesson-level statements written by the teacher in the lesson plan. SLOs must be directly derived from the relevant GLO, limited in number (typically 2–4 per lesson), and achievable within the lesson period. This is what most CBC teachers write daily.

Example: "By the end of the lesson, the learner should be able to solve two-step linear equations using the balance method."
🔑 Why ELOs matter in CBC
  • They guide the teacher's choice of learning activities, resources, and assessment tools from the 13 KICD instruments.
  • They define exactly what evidence will show that learning has occurred — making formative assessment purposeful.
  • They ensure alignment between curriculum design (KICD), instruction (teacher), and assessment (TSC portfolios and KPSEA/KJSEA/KCSE).
  • They protect learners from teacher-centred lessons where teaching happens but learning is not verified.
  • They form the starting point of backward design — you plan assessment first, then design activities that lead to the outcome.
// 02 · Characteristics

Characteristics of a Well-Written ELO

KICD's curriculum framework prescribes that every Expected Learning Outcome must meet the following quality standards to be considered valid and usable.

🎯

Learner-Centred

Must begin with "The learner should be able to…" or "By the end of the lesson, the learner will…" — never describe what the teacher will do.

👁️

Observable

Must use an action verb from Bloom's Taxonomy that can be seen and measured — avoid vague verbs like "understand", "know", or "appreciate".

📏

Measurable

Must specify a performance criterion or degree (e.g. "correctly", "at least 3 out of 4", "with reference to a diagram") so teachers can assess attainment.

✅

Achievable

Must be realistic for the learner's level (grade, age, prior knowledge) and attainable within the allotted lesson time — not too easy, not too demanding.

🔗

Aligned to GLO

Every SLO must be a logical narrowing of the relevant General Learning Outcome from the curriculum design — direct curriculum alignment is non-negotiable in CBC.

🌍

Contextualised

Good ELOs situate learning in a real, familiar, or locally relevant context — connecting classroom knowledge to the community, career world, or everyday life.

🔑

Competency-Based

Must reflect at least one of the 7 CBC core competencies (e.g. Communication, Critical Thinking, Digital Literacy) — not just content recall.

🔢

Appropriately Numbered

KICD lesson plan format recommends 2–4 SLOs per lesson. Too few risk shallow coverage; too many indicate a lesson that is overloaded and undeliverable in one period.

M·01

The VOC Model — Verb · Object · Context

The simplest and most widely used ELO writing model in Kenya's CBC classrooms

Most Used

The VOC model produces a learning outcome in a single, well-structured sentence containing exactly three elements: a Bloom's Taxonomy Verb (the observable action), an Object (the specific content or concept), and a Context (the real-world or classroom condition). It is the preferred model for daily CBC lesson plans at all grade levels.

=
Expected Learning Outcome
V
Verb (Action Word)
An observable, measurable action verb drawn from Bloom's Taxonomy (e.g. identify, explain, classify, design, evaluate, construct, compare). The verb determines the cognitive level of the outcome and must be appropriate for the learner's grade and prior knowledge. Vague verbs like "understand", "know", "appreciate", or "be aware of" are not acceptable in CBC ELOs because they cannot be directly observed or measured.
O
Object (Content)
The specific topic, concept, skill, or knowledge the learner will act upon. This is directly derived from the CBC curriculum sub-strand. It should be narrow enough to be achievable in one lesson — not an entire topic. For example, "fractions" is too broad; "equivalent fractions using a number line" is appropriate.
C
Context (Condition)
The real-world, local, or classroom situation in which learning will be demonstrated. The context grounds the outcome in CBC's emphasis on contextualised, meaningful learning. It connects the classroom to the community and gives the learner a reason to learn. Context can be a physical setting, a specific resource, a social situation, or a career scenario.

Worked Examples Across Subjects

📌 VOC Example
Subject: Mathematics · Grade 8 · Strand: Numbers · Sub-strand: Fractions

"Calculate [V] the percentage increase of commodity prices [O] using data from the local market survey [C]."

Verb: Calculate — a Bloom's Application level verb, observable through workings and final answer.
Object: Percentage increase of commodity prices — specific enough for one lesson; derived from the GLO on percentage applications.
Context: Local market survey data — connects to real community life, makes learning relevant and CBC-aligned.
Subject: Integrated Science · Grade 7 · Strand: Living Things · Sub-strand: Classification

"Classify [V] ten locally found organisms [O] into the five kingdoms using observable features [C]."

Verb: Classify — Bloom's Comprehension/Analysis; observable through the classification chart produced.
Object: Locally found organisms — narrow and specific, achievable in one lesson using field specimens.
Context: Using observable features — situates learning in hands-on inquiry, supporting CBC's PBL approach.
Subject: English · Grade 9 · Strand: Writing · Sub-strand: Functional Writing

"Write [V] a formal letter of complaint [O] in response to a school canteen quality issue [C]."

Verb: Write — Bloom's Application; observable through the written letter produced by the learner.
Object: A formal letter of complaint — specific genre, not just "a letter" in general.
Context: School canteen quality issue — a familiar, age-relevant situation that gives the task authentic purpose.
Subject: Computer Science · Grade 10 · Strand: Programming · Sub-strand: Algorithms

"Design [V] a flowchart for a sorting algorithm [O] to solve a real inventory management scenario [C]."

Verb: Design — Bloom's Synthesis/Creation; observable through the drawn/digital flowchart submitted.
Object: Flowchart for a sorting algorithm — precise enough for one lesson; maps directly to the STEM pathway CS curriculum.
Context: Inventory management scenario — real-world business application, reinforcing Digital Literacy competency.
Subject: Social Studies · Grade 7 · Strand: Our Country Kenya · Sub-strand: Physical Features

"Label [V] the major relief features of Kenya [O] on an outline map of East Africa [C]."

Verb: Label — Bloom's Knowledge/Comprehension; observable through the completed annotated map.
Object: Major relief features of Kenya — specific sub-strand content, not all of geography.
Context: An outline map of East Africa — specifies the instructional tool and connects to regional geography.
⚠️ VOC Model — Teacher Tips
  • Never split the VOC into three separate bullet points. Write it as one complete, grammatically correct sentence.
  • The context does not have to be a physical place — it can be a tool ("using a calculator"), a source ("from the textbook data"), or a scenario ("given a patient case study").
  • For affective/values outcomes, VOC still works: "Demonstrate [V] respect for diverse cultural practices [O] during a class community mapping activity [C]."
  • One lesson may have 2–4 VOC-based SLOs — one at knowledge level, one at application, and optionally one at values/attitudes level (KSV/A alignment).
M·02

The ABCD Model — Audience · Behaviour · Condition · Degree

A four-component instructional design model widely used in curriculum planning and teacher training

Curriculum Design

The ABCD model, developed in instructional design theory and adopted by KICD for curriculum development and teacher training contexts, extends the VOC by explicitly naming the learner (Audience) and adding a performance standard (Degree). It is especially useful when writing outcomes for diverse learning populations or when a specific pass criterion must be stated.

+
BBehaviour
+
CCondition
+
DDegree
=
Expected Learning Outcome
A
Audience
The specific group of learners the outcome is written for. In CBC lesson plans, this is usually "the learner" or "Grade 8 learners" or for differentiated instruction, "learners with advanced literacy skills". Being specific about the audience helps the teacher plan differentiated activities for diverse learners — a key CBC pedagogical requirement.
B
Behaviour
The observable, measurable action the learner will perform — equivalent to the Verb + Object in the VOC model. This is the core of the ELO. It must be stated using a precise Bloom's Taxonomy verb and specific content. The behaviour must be demonstrable through one of the 13 KICD assessment tools.
C
Condition
The given materials, tools, constraints, or context under which the behaviour will be demonstrated. This could be resources provided ("given a periodic table"), constraints applied ("without a calculator"), or the setting specified ("during a group discussion"). Conditions make the outcome assessable and specific.
D
Degree
The criterion or standard against which the learner's performance is judged — the minimum acceptable level. It answers: "How well must the learner perform?" Examples: "with at least 80% accuracy", "correctly naming 5 out of 6", "without any grammatical errors", "scoring at least 3 on a 4-point rubric". This element makes formative assessment concrete and actionable.
📌 ABCD Examples
Mathematics (Grade 9 · Algebra):
"Grade 9 learners [A] will solve simultaneous linear equations [B] using the substitution method, given two equations and a worked example [C], correctly solving at least 3 out of 4 problems [D]."

Biology (Grade 11 · STEM · Cell Division):
"The learner [A] will draw and label the four stages of mitosis [B] from memory, without reference to a textbook [C], correctly labelling all stages with accurate descriptions of chromosomal behaviour [D]."

Kiswahili (Grade 7 · Uandishi):
"Wanafunzi wa Darasa la 7 [A] wataweza kuandika barua rasmi [B] kwa kutumia muundo sahihi wa KICD [C], wakipata angalau alama 12 kati ya 20 kwenye rubric ya tathmini [D]."
💡 When to prefer ABCD over VOC
  • When writing outcomes for curriculum documents, schemes of work, or teacher induction materials — the Degree makes accountability explicit.
  • When planning differentiated instruction — stating the Audience allows writing separate outcomes for learners at different ability levels.
  • When designing summative assessment tasks where a pass mark or rubric threshold must be stated in the task brief.
  • The ABCD model is the recommended format for CBC Teacher Performance Standards documentation submitted to TSC.
M·03

The SMART Model — Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Time-bound

A goal-setting framework adapted for curriculum and lesson planning in CBC contexts

Goal-Setting

Originally developed in management and goal-setting literature, the SMART framework is used in CBC to audit and verify the quality of already-written ELOs, and as a planning checklist during unit and scheme-of-work development. A well-written ELO should satisfy all five SMART criteria simultaneously.

S
Specific
The outcome must target one clear, well-defined skill, concept, or value — not a broad topic. "The learner will understand photosynthesis" fails this criterion. "The learner will explain the role of chlorophyll in light-dependent reactions" passes. Specificity allows the teacher to plan one focused activity and select one appropriate assessment tool.
M
Measurable
Evidence of achievement must be collectable using one of the 13 KICD assessment tools (Observation Schedule, Rubric, Checklist, Written Test, Portfolio, etc.). The outcome must describe a behaviour that produces a visible, audible, or tangible product that the teacher can assess. If you cannot design a fair assessment for it, the outcome is not measurable.
A
Achievable
The outcome must be realistic for the learner's current developmental stage, prior knowledge, available resources, and lesson time. An outcome requiring a Grade 7 learner to "develop a fully functional mobile application" in one period is not achievable. Achievability also considers equity — outcomes must be reachable by all learners, with differentiated support where needed.
R
Relevant
The outcome must directly contribute to the attainment of the CBC sub-strand's General Learning Outcome and at least one core competency. It must also be meaningful to the learner's life, community, or future career pathway. Relevance is central to CBC's value of contextualised learning and community connection.
T
Time-bound
The outcome must be stated within a specific time frame — "by the end of this lesson", "within this unit", or "by the end of Grade 9". This prevents vague, open-ended outcomes and enables teachers to plan lesson pacing, assess progress within the KICD term-by-term content coverage, and sequence learning progressively across units.
📌 SMART Audit Example — History & Citizenship (Grade 8)
Original ELO (unaudited): "The learner will understand the colonial period in Kenya."

SMART audit:
❌ S: "Colonial period" is far too broad — multiple lessons' worth of content.
❌ M: "Understand" cannot be measured with any KICD tool.
✅ A: Colonial history is grade-appropriate content.
❌ R: No connection stated to core competencies or the learner's community.
❌ T: No time frame specified.

Revised ELO (SMART-compliant):
"By the end of this lesson, the learner will compare the economic impacts of colonial land policies on Kenyan communities using textbook sources and local oral history, identifying at least three specific effects."
M·04

Mager's Performance Objective Model — Performance · Condition · Criterion

Robert Mager's classic model, emphasising strict performance specifications and measurable criteria

Technical & TVET

Robert Mager introduced this model in Preparing Instructional Objectives (1962), and it remains one of the most precise ELO writing frameworks adopted in KICD's assessment and curriculum documentation. It is particularly suited to technical, vocational, and psychomotor outcomes — making it ideal for CBC's STEM, Arts & Sports, and TVET pathways where specific performance standards must be stated.

PPerformance
+
CCondition
+
CrCriterion
=
Performance Objective
P
Performance
The specific, observable act the learner will perform — expressed as a verb + content phrase. It describes the terminal behaviour: what the learner will actually do at the end of the lesson. For CBC, this must be a Bloom's Taxonomy action verb. The performance is the heart of the objective — the other two components simply sharpen it.
C
Condition
The specific situation, tools, resources, constraints, or stimuli under which the performance will be demonstrated. For TVET and STEM subjects, conditions are very precise: "given a vernier calliper and a steel cylinder", "using Python 3 and a standard IDE", "with access to a periodic table but no other reference". This ensures fairness and reproducibility of assessment.
Cr
Criterion
The explicit standard that defines acceptable performance — the benchmark for "good enough". It can be expressed as accuracy ("within ±0.5mm"), completeness ("all 8 safety checks"), speed ("within 10 minutes"), or quality ("achieving a score of 3 or higher on a 4-level analytic rubric"). The criterion directly feeds into CBC's competency grading and formative assessment records.
📌 Mager's Model Examples
Technical Drawing / Design & Technology (Grade 10 · STEM):
"Draw a first-angle orthographic projection of a cylindrical bracket [P], given a 3D isometric view and drawing instruments but without a template [C], with all dimensions correctly labelled to ±0.5mm accuracy and full title block completed [Cr]."

Physical Education / Sports Science (Grade 11 · Arts & Sports):
"Demonstrate the correct technique for a basketball lay-up shot [P], from a live-ball dribble situation in a 3-vs-2 defensive drill [C], achieving a successful basket in at least 4 out of 6 attempts [Cr]."

Agriculture (Grade 9 · STEM/Practical):
"Prepare a seedbed for transplanting tomato seedlings [P], using locally available materials and without supervisor assistance [C], completing all 7 steps on the KICD practical checklist within 40 minutes [Cr]."
🔧 Mager Model — Best Use Cases in CBC
  • Psychomotor domain outcomes (lab experiments, sports techniques, art production, music performance).
  • TVET pathway assessments where industry-standard precision is required in lesson planning.
  • Practical examination preparation — where exact conditions and criteria must be communicated to learners in advance.
  • Writing rubric anchor descriptors — the Criterion directly translates into rubric performance level descriptors.
// 07 · KSV/A Framework

The KSV/A Framework — Knowledge · Skills · Values/Attitudes

The KSV/A framework is KICD's three-domain taxonomy for categorising what CBC learning outcomes target. Every well-rounded CBC lesson should include outcomes across all three domains — not just cognitive knowledge.

Why KICD Uses KSV/A

The 8-4-4 curriculum was criticised for producing learners rich in knowledge but lacking in practical skills and positive values and attitudes. CBC addresses this directly through the KSV/A framework — requiring that lesson planning, instruction, and assessment address all three learning domains in every sub-strand. A lesson plan with only knowledge-level ELOs is considered incomplete under KICD standards.

K

Knowledge

Cognitive Domain

What the learner will know, understand, and recall — facts, concepts, principles, theories, and information. Knowledge outcomes target the mind and are assessed through written tests, oral Q&A, interviews, and questionnaires. They correspond to the lower-to-middle levels of Bloom's Taxonomy (Remembering → Analysing).

Bloom's Levels 1–4
State the three states of matter and their properties
Identify the organs of the digestive system
Recall the causes of World War I
Define osmosis and give two examples
Name the layers of the atmosphere in order
S

Skills

Psychomotor + Higher Cognitive

What the learner will be able to do, apply, create, and perform — practical, communication, analytical, and technical skills. Skills outcomes are assessed through projects, portfolios, observation schedules, peer assessment, and practical demonstrations. They correspond to the upper levels of Bloom's (Applying → Creating).

Bloom's Levels 3–6
Construct a circuit diagram using correct symbols
Write a persuasive speech on climate change
Design a database schema for a school library
Perform a forward roll with correct technique
Use a microscope to prepare and observe a slide
V/A

Values & Attitudes

Affective Domain

What the learner will internalise, value, believe, and consistently demonstrate in behaviour — moral, civic, and interpersonal dispositions. Values/Attitudes outcomes are often the most neglected but are central to CBC's citizenship competency. Assessed through anecdotal records, self-assessment, observation schedules, and portfolios over time.

Affective Domain
Demonstrate teamwork during group projects
Show respect for different cultural practices
Appreciate the importance of environmental conservation
Develop a habit of punctuality and time management
Exhibit honesty when reporting laboratory results

KSV/A Balance in a CBC Lesson Plan

KICD recommends that a standard lesson plan (2–4 ELOs) includes at least one Knowledge outcome, one Skills outcome, and one Values/Attitudes outcome. This ensures learners develop as whole persons — cognitively, practically, and morally — fulfilling the CBC holistic education mandate.

✅ Well-Balanced Lesson (Gr 8 Sci)
K: State the products of photosynthesis. S: Conduct a leaf starch test experiment. V/A: Appreciate the role of plants in ecosystem balance.
⚠️ Knowledge-Heavy (Imbalanced)
K1: Define photosynthesis. K2: List the reactants. K3: State the equation. — All three outcomes are at Bloom's Level 1–2 with no skills or values dimension. Common in 8-4-4 legacy teaching.
💡 Differentiating V/A Outcomes
Values/Attitudes outcomes develop over time — write them as "The learner will develop/appreciate/show/demonstrate" and assess through observation schedules and anecdotal records across the unit, not just in one lesson.
// 08 · Bloom's Taxonomy

Bloom's Revised Taxonomy — The Verb Source for ELOs

Bloom's Revised Taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001 — a revision of Bloom's 1956 original) is the primary source for action verbs in CBC ELO writing. KICD explicitly requires teachers to select verbs from Bloom's levels appropriate to the learner's grade and lesson purpose.

The taxonomy organises cognitive learning into six hierarchical levels — from simple recall at the base to complex creative synthesis at the apex. CBC's competency-based approach demands outcomes across all six levels, with higher-order outcomes (Analysis, Evaluation, Creation) especially valued because they reflect real-world problem-solving and not just memorisation.

🚫 Bloom's Forbidden Verbs in CBC ELOs

The following verbs are not acceptable in CBC ELO writing because they describe internal, unobservable mental states that cannot be directly assessed using any of the 13 KICD tools:

Understand Know Appreciate Be aware of Think about Feel Realise Believe Enjoy Learn Study Be familiar with

Exception: Values/Attitudes (V/A) outcomes may use words like "appreciate" or "develop" only when combined with observable behavioural indicators — e.g. "Appreciate the importance of water conservation by consistently participating in the school water-saving campaign."

// 09 · Theoretical Foundations

Learning Theories Underpinning CBC ELOs

The ELO writing models and the CBC curriculum are not arbitrary — they are grounded in well-established educational theories. Understanding these theories helps teachers write better ELOs and design more effective lessons.

🧱

Constructivism

Vygotsky · Piaget · Bruner

Learners actively construct knowledge by connecting new experiences to prior knowledge. Knowledge is not passively received — it is personally built through interaction with the environment and social engagement.

CBC requires ELOs to build progressively from prior learning — "identify before classify before analyse". The Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky) directly informs the Achievable criterion in SMART outcomes and the Degree in Mager's model.
🌱

Experiential Learning Theory

David Kolb (1984)

Learning is a cycle: Concrete Experience → Reflective Observation → Abstract Conceptualisation → Active Experimentation. Learners learn best when they experience, reflect, conceptualise, and then test in new contexts.

CBC's Project-Based Learning (PBL) and Community Service Learning (CSL) are built on Kolb's cycle. ELOs for project and portfolio tasks should address all four stages of the cycle across a unit — not just conceptualisation alone.
🔗

Social Learning Theory

Albert Bandura (1977)

Learners learn by observing, modelling, and imitating others in social contexts. Self-efficacy — a learner's belief in their own ability — is a crucial predictor of learning success and persistence.

Self-Efficacy is one of CBC's 7 core competencies. ELOs must be achievable enough to build self-confidence, especially for lower-ability learners. Peer and self-assessment tools directly leverage Bandura's modelling principles.
🎯

Competency-Based Education (CBE) Theory

Benjamin Bloom · Grant Wiggins

Learners must demonstrate mastery of defined competencies before progressing — time is variable, but learning standards are fixed. This contrasts directly with the 8-4-4 time-fixed, standard-variable model.

CBC is Kenya's implementation of CBE. ELOs serve as the "mastery definitions" for each lesson and unit. The 60:40 (coursework:exam) split ensures competency is assessed continuously — not just at the terminal exam.
🔄

Backward Design (UbD)

Wiggins & McTighe (1998)

Effective curriculum design begins with the end in mind: first define the desired outcomes and assessment evidence, then design the learning activities. This is the opposite of "coverage-first" teaching.

KICD's lesson plan format follows backward design: ELOs are written first, assessment tools selected second, and learning activities planned last. This is why writing good ELOs is the single most important lesson planning skill in CBC.
🧠

Multiple Intelligences Theory

Howard Gardner (1983)

Learners have multiple distinct intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and naturalist. Schools historically over-valued only the first two.

CBC's three-pathway structure (STEM, Social Sciences, Arts & Sports) and the KSV/A framework directly honour multiple intelligences. ELOs should be written across all three domains so that learners with different intelligences can demonstrate competency in different ways.

// 11 · Common Mistakes

Common ELO Writing Mistakes — and How to Fix Them

These are the most frequently observed errors in CBC lesson plans inspected by KICD curriculum support officers and TSC quality assurance teams.

❌ Wrong

"The learner will understand photosynthesis."

Problem: "Understand" is an unobservable verb; no assessment tool can directly measure it.

✅ Corrected

"The learner will explain the process of photosynthesis using a labelled diagram of a leaf cell."

Fix: Replaced with observable verb "explain"; object and context added.

❌ Wrong

"The learner will study all of algebra."

Problem: Not specific; covers multiple sub-strands and many lessons' worth of content.

✅ Corrected

"The learner will solve two-step linear equations involving one variable using the balancing method."

Fix: Narrowed to a specific skill with a clear method specified.

❌ Wrong

"The teacher will teach the water cycle using a diagram."

Problem: Describes teacher activity, not learner outcome — violates the learner-centred principle.

✅ Corrected

"The learner will trace the water cycle and label its four stages on a blank diagram."

Fix: Re-centred on the learner; two observable outcomes clearly stated.

❌ Wrong

"The learner will write an essay, do a group presentation, complete a portfolio, conduct a survey, and create a model about climate change."

Problem: Five separate tasks in one lesson — unachievable and unfocused.

✅ Corrected

"The learner will write a structured 3-paragraph opinion essay on the effects of climate change on Kenyan farming communities."

Fix: One achievable task with specific parameters; others moved to subsequent lessons.

❌ Wrong (All K-level)

ELO 1: "Define erosion." ELO 2: "State three causes of erosion." ELO 3: "Name two types of soil erosion."

Problem: All three outcomes are at Bloom's Level 1 (Remember) — no skills or values dimension; typical 8-4-4 legacy planning.

✅ KSV/A-Balanced

K: "Define soil erosion and identify its causes." S: "Construct a soil conservation model using local materials." V/A: "Show commitment to soil conservation practices in the school compound."

Fix: One outcome per KSV/A domain; progressive Bloom's levels.

// 12 · Writing Guide

Step-by-Step: How to Write CBC ELOs for Your Lesson Plan

Follow this seven-step process every time you write Expected Learning Outcomes for a CBC lesson plan — from curriculum consultation to final verification.

01
Consult the KICD Curriculum Design Document

Open the relevant KICD curriculum design document for your subject, grade, strand, and sub-strand. Identify the General Learning Outcome (GLO) for the sub-strand you are teaching. Your SLOs must be directly derived from this GLO — never invented independently. The curriculum document is your non-negotiable starting point.

02
Determine What Can Be Achieved in One Lesson Period

Break the GLO into smaller, lesson-sized chunks. Ask: "What is the single most important thing learners must be able to do after this 40-minute lesson that they could not do before?" This discipline prevents overloaded lesson plans and ensures realistic ELOs. A single lesson should contribute to — but not fully achieve — a broad GLO.

03
Plan for KSV/A Coverage

Decide which domains you will address in this lesson — Knowledge, Skills, and/or Values/Attitudes. Plan at least one outcome per domain. Remember: it is acceptable to have a stronger Knowledge + Skills lesson if a previous lesson covered Values/Attitudes deeply — balance can be achieved across a unit, not necessarily every single period.

04
Select Your Bloom's Taxonomy Verbs

For each planned outcome, choose one precise, observable Bloom's verb at the correct cognitive level for the learner's grade and prior knowledge. Progression within a unit should move upward through Bloom's levels — start lower-order early in a unit and progress to higher-order by the end. Avoid defaulting to Levels 1–2 only.

05
Choose Your Writing Model and Draft the ELO

Select the most appropriate model — VOC for daily lessons; ABCD for differentiated or formal planning; Mager's for technical/practical outcomes. Draft each ELO as one complete, grammatically correct sentence. Begin each with the standard stem: "By the end of the lesson, the learner should be able to…" followed immediately by your Bloom's verb.

06
Run the SMART Audit on Each ELO

Check each draft ELO against the five SMART criteria. Ask: Is it Specific enough? Can I measure it with a KICD tool? Is it Achievable for all my learners? Is it Relevant to the sub-strand and core competencies? Is the time frame stated? Edit until all five criteria are satisfied. This step prevents common errors before you finalise the lesson plan.

07
Select Your Assessment Tool(s) and Design Backward

For each finalised ELO, select the appropriate KICD assessment tool (e.g. Rubric for the Skills outcome; Observation Schedule for the Values/Attitudes outcome; Written Test for the Knowledge outcome). Then design your learning activities specifically to prepare learners to succeed in those assessments. This is backward design — assessment drives instruction, not the reverse.

📋 Complete Worked Example — Grade 8 Mathematics
Sub-strand: Algebra — Solving Linear Equations (Grades 7–9 Curriculum)
GLO: The learner should be able to apply algebraic methods to model and solve real-life situations.

ELO 1 (K · VOC · Bloom's L2): "By the end of the lesson, the learner should be able to explain the meaning of a variable and a coefficient in a linear equation using examples from daily spending."
Assessment tool: Oral Questions & Answers

ELO 2 (S · VOC · Bloom's L3): "By the end of the lesson, the learner should be able to solve two-step linear equations involving positive integers using the balancing method, given three word problems based on market pricing."
Assessment tool: Written Test (3-question exercise)

ELO 3 (V/A · VOC · Affective): "By the end of the lesson, the learner should be able to demonstrate patience and perseverance when solving multi-step problems during pair work."
Assessment tool: Observation Schedule (teacher records behaviour during pair work)
